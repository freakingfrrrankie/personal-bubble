The research began with a search for what was called digital flesh: an attempt to imagine what kind of body might inhabit digital space. This was expected to lead toward another form of the body — perhaps its representation, extension or informational counterpart. Instead, the practical research gradually made the distinction between body and digital space itself difficult to maintain.

What emerged was not another body, but a series of relationships through which bodies, technologies and environments become perceptible and consequential to one another: a gesture becomes constrained by a tool; a voice becomes a trace; a doorway crossing becomes a count; a count becomes a rule; a rule returns as sound or spatial movement. Across these translations, neither body nor technology remains entirely on one side of the relation.

If digital flesh still has meaning within this research, it therefore names not the body translated into data, but the relational tissue through which bodily and technological conditions continually pass into and alter one another.
