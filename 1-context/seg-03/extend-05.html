This also clarifies the distinction established in the previous section:

Embodiment describes how a relationship enters bodily experience. Environment describes what happens when such relationships become part of the conditions within which bodily experience takes place.

Once a technological system no longer simply responds to an occasional encounter but operates as part of the ongoing conditions of a space, how does that environment perceive the body in the first place?

Tim Ingold offers another way of thinking about this extension. An environment is not simply a container that surrounds already completed bodies and things. Bodies and environments take form through their ongoing relations—through moving, perceiving, responding and inhabiting.

This is why simply being present is not enough. The lettering guide can lie beside me all day. A technological system can remain switched on all night. Persistence alone does not make either one environmental.

The question is whether it begins to participate in the conditions of what I can do. Can I see this? Can I move here? Can I withdraw? Can I remain unnoticed? What possibilities are available to me before I have even decided to act? This is the sense in which I use environment throughout this research.

Environment is not simply what surrounds the body, but the conditions within which bodily experience takes place. Technology becomes environmental when it operates as part of these conditions rather than as a discrete tool one occasionally reaches for. The distinction is small, but important.

Embodiment describes how a relationship enters bodily experience. Environment describes what happens when such relationships become part of the conditions within which bodily experience takes place.

I can embody a tool without living inside its conditions. But when technology becomes environmental, simply putting the tool down may no longer be enough. If this technological environment can sense, calculate and respond to my presence—what, exactly, does it think my body is?
